Why $1,000 outgrows $10,000 by so much here

At the 99% threshold, a $1,000 run and a $10,000 run land close together (7.7% vs 6.6% CAGR). At 70% they diverge enormously — 85.5% CAGR at $1,000 vs 66.4% at $10,000, on almost the identical trade population (1,948 taken / 345 flips at $1,000 vs 1,965 taken / 351 flips at $10,000, from the same 3,819‑signal pool). The outcomes aren't different because different bets were won or lost — they're different because of how often a fixed, real‑world liquidity ceiling throttles the position size below what Kelly actually wants.

Checked directly rather than assumed: at $10,000, 21.8% of taken trades were sized below 1% of equity despite the model wanting more (depth‑capped by the public trade tape's real transactable volume near each crossing, a fixed dollar ceiling, not a percentage). At $1,000, only 16.7% of trades hit that same throttle. A five‑point gap in how often positions get clipped sounds small, but compounded across ~2,000 sequential trades over 3.5 years — where getting a fuller position size early lets that edge compound for years longer — it snowballs into a very large gap in final equity. This threshold takes far more trades than the 99% case (3,819 signals vs 2,367), which is exactly why the effect is so much more visible here: more trades means more compounding cycles for a small per‑trade advantage to multiply through.

One important thing this does not mean: that a small account has some inherent structural edge worth chasing on its own. It means a fixed, historically‑estimated liquidity ceiling (itself only a proxy, since real order‑book depth for resolved markets isn't recoverable) binds less against a smaller bankroll's smaller absolute position sizes — a real effect in this backtest, but one that would erode as an account grows, and one that's only as trustworthy as the liquidity proxy itself.

Reading this correctly. This is a historical backtest on resolved Polymarket markets, not a live track record — no trades were placed. Fill prices are the last observed CLOB snapshot before entry, not a guaranteed executable quote. At this looser threshold the Kelly sizing also uses the same flip‑rate prior calibrated for the ~99%+ population, which is badly miscalibrated for 70%'s much higher true flip rate (12.5% vs under 0.2%) — sizing here is more aggressive early in the backtest than a properly threshold‑aware prior would allow.
