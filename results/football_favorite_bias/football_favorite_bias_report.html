Does the Favorite-Longshot Edge Survive a Switch to a Sharp Bookmaker?

Polymarket's Final-1% strategy found a persistent, exploitable edge in backing extreme favorites on a thin, retail-heavy prediction market. This re-runs the same 70%-threshold, walk-forward Kelly methodology on 41,314 football matches across 9 European leagues (2012/13–2024/25), using free closing-line odds from football-data.co.uk, anchored to Pinnacle — the sports-betting industry's reference "sharp" book.

Source football-data.co.uk, free CSV, no account Price Pinnacle closing odds (vig included, no fee added on top) Method identical run_sim / run_flat_sim engines from the Polymarket project
×

The edge does not survive an out-of-sample split

Backed on the full 2012–2025 sample, the 70% threshold strategy looks like a real discovery (quarter-Kelly turns $10,000 into $20,235). But splitting the sample in half by date — each half re-starting its walk-forward belief from a cold prior, so nothing leaks across the split — shows the "edge" was +1.02pp in 2012–2019 and -0.74pp in 2019–2025: it flips sign. That is the signature of a sample-period artifact, not a structural mispricing, and it's the opposite of what the Polymarket project found when it ran the same check.

1. Does the raw closing price already predict the outcome?

Before any staking model: at each threshold, is the favorite's realized win rate above or below what the vig-inclusive closing price implies? A trader profits only where win rate > price — anywhere else, the bookmaker's built-in margin (mean overround 2.59% across this sample) is enough on its own to beat the bettor.

2. Threshold sweep: quarter-Kelly vs. flat 1%-of-equity

4. Split-sample robustness — the check that overturns Section 3

Each half below re-starts the per-league walk-forward belief from a cold Beta(5,15) prior at the split date, so nothing about the "edge" in one half is informed by the other. A structural mispricing should show up in both; an edge that appears in one lucky decade and vanishes in the next is not something to trade live money on. (Same spirit as this repo's leakage_check.py for the Polymarket project, applied here as a train/test time split instead of a lookahead check.)

5. Per-league breakdown (70% threshold, full sample)

The pooled edge is small and, per Section 4, unstable over time — but it's also not spread evenly across leagues. Portugal, Spain and Italy show a consistent positive edge; Germany and the Netherlands show a consistent negative one. That heterogeneity is a more plausible place to look for a real, narrower effect than the pooled 70% number, but testing that would need its own split-sample check per league before it's trustworthy — not done here, flagged as the natural next step.
